Why You Should Evaluate UAS Platforms Like a Special Operations Mission

Forge & Flight Holdings, Inc.

In Special Operations, we didn't brief a mission and then execute it. We rehearsed it.

Physical rehearsals at full scale when the environment allowed. Reduced-scale mockups when it didn't. Sand tables when those weren't available. The rehearsal wasn't about memorizing a script or walking through a sequence of events. It was about building a mental model detailed enough that decision points — the moments when conditions diverged from the plan — became automatic rather than deliberate.

By the time we executed, we'd already lived through the operation multiple times. The unexpected became less disorienting because we'd stress-tested our assumptions in a controlled environment first. That discipline is directly applicable to UAS platform evaluation, and most procurement processes skip it entirely.

The Demo Problem

The standard UAS procurement evaluation looks like this: the vendor conducts a demonstration flight at a location of their choosing, with conditions that favor the platform, operated by their most experienced pilot, with the payload configuration that shows the best performance numbers. The platform performs well. It looks capable. The evaluation team is impressed. The procurement proceeds.

And then the platform arrives in the operational environment and the numbers don't match the demo. This isn't necessarily vendor deception. Vendors demonstrate their platforms under favorable conditions because that's rational. The problem is that procurement processes accept the demonstration as operationally representative when it isn't. A demonstration tells you what the platform can do under ideal conditions. It tells you very little about what the platform can do under your conditions, with your operators, against your mission requirements.

What Rehearsal Looks Like in Platform Evaluation

The operational equivalent of rehearsal in procurement is evaluation against your actual mission profile — not the vendor's demonstration profile.

  • Your operational area, not their demo site. Terrain affects line-of-sight. Urban environments affect RF performance. Altitude affects power consumption and endurance.
  • Your payloads, not theirs. Every payload adds weight, changes aerodynamics, affects power consumption, and may introduce RF interference.
  • Your operators, not their factory pilot. A factory test pilot with hundreds of hours on a specific platform will demonstrate performance your freshly-trained operators cannot immediately replicate.
  • Your environmental conditions, not a calm morning. Wind loading, temperature extremes, and precipitation all affect platform performance and operator workload.

What You Discover in Rehearsal

The value of rehearsal — in operations and in procurement — is what you learn about the gap between assumptions and reality. In platform evaluation done right, you discover:

  • The payload you require adds more weight than the spec sheet suggested, cutting endurance by 20%
  • Line-of-sight in your operational area is blocked by terrain at a range shorter than the advertised control link distance
  • Your operators need significantly more familiarization time before they're confident in the platform's emergency procedures
  • The maintenance cycle at your operational tempo requires more time than the post-flight inspection procedure suggests

None of these are necessarily disqualifying. Most of them are manageable if you discover them during evaluation rather than during an operational deployment. The ones that are disqualifying are far better identified before contract award than after.

The Discipline Required

The challenge is that thorough operational evaluation costs more time and resources than accepting a vendor demonstration. In procurement environments with timeline pressure, that cost feels prohibitive. The comparison to make is not "evaluation cost vs. zero." The comparison is "evaluation cost vs. the cost of procuring the wrong platform and discovering it after contract award."

In Special Operations, the rehearsal investment was never questioned because the alternative — discovering your plan's flaws during the operation rather than before it — was clearly more costly. The same logic applies. Evaluate at operational fidelity before you commit.
