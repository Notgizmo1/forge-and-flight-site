Target Fixation: The Decision-Making Failure That Costs Agencies Millions

Forge & Flight Holdings, Inc.

In Special Operations, we had a name for it: target fixation.

It happens when an operator — or an entire element — locks onto a single course of action with such certainty that they stop processing contradicting information. The mission plan becomes the reality, even when the situation on the ground is telling a different story. Actions become automatic. Dissenting observations get filtered out. And then something goes wrong that everyone should have seen coming.

Twenty years in Special Operations, and I watched this failure mode play out more than once. Not because the operators were incompetent. Because they were experienced, confident, and deeply committed to a plan they'd put significant effort into developing. Commitment to a plan is an asset until the moment it becomes an inability to update the plan.

The Same Failure in Aerospace Procurement

I see an operationally identical failure mode in government UAS procurement, and the cost is measured in warehouses full of expensive equipment that nobody is flying.

Here's how it typically unfolds: A program office identifies a requirement. Someone sees a vendor demo early in the process — often before requirements are formally written. The platform looks capable. The vendor relationship develops. Requirements get written, and they happen to align closely with what the early-preferred platform offers. Competing solutions never receive a serious evaluation because the evaluation criteria were shaped by the solution already favored.

The platform is procured. It underperforms in the operational environment. The specification looked good in the conference room and failed in the field. Nobody wants to be the person who says the decision was wrong, so the platform sits. The procurement officer wasn't incompetent. They were committed to a plan. The failure mode is the same.

The Special Operations Fix: Red-Teaming

In operational planning, we addressed target fixation through deliberate red-teaming. Before any significant operation, someone was assigned to argue against the plan. Not to be obstructive, but to surface the assumptions the planning team had stopped questioning because they'd lived with the plan long enough to mistake familiarity for validation.

The best commanders I served under actively sought out the red team's analysis. They understood that a plan stress-tested by an honest adversarial review is stronger than a plan everyone agrees with because nobody wanted to be the one who pushed back. The discipline required isn't intellectual — it's cultural. You have to build an environment where surfacing a flaw before execution is valued more than protecting a plan after it's been briefed up the chain.

The Procurement Application

The structural equivalent in acquisition is straightforward: evaluation criteria established before vendor engagement, not after. Set your operational requirements. Define your evaluation criteria. Then engage the market. When you've seen a compelling demo before the requirements are written, require yourself to articulate why you're selecting what you're selecting in terms that could be applied equally to any competing solution. If you can't, your criteria may be describing a solution rather than a requirement.

Require an honest internal challenge of the leading option before contract award. Assign someone to find the gaps. If they can't find any, the platform is probably genuinely strong. If they find several and the process continues anyway, you've documented a decision that will be difficult to defend after the fact.

What This Looks Like in Practice

The agencies I've seen get UAS procurement right share one characteristic: they evaluate platforms against mission profiles, not platform specifications. They define the mission — the operational environment, the payload requirements, the maintenance constraints, the operator skill level — and then ask which platform serves that mission best.

That question is harder to answer than "which platform scored highest on the spec sheet." It requires evaluation in the actual operational environment, with actual operators, against actual mission requirements. It requires being willing to discover that the early-preferred option isn't the right answer.

In Special Operations, we called that intellectual honesty. It was the difference between a successful mission and a debrief you'd rather forget. The same discipline applies here.
