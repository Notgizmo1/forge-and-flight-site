Annual OPSEC training teaches operators that threats exist. It does not teach them to do anything about those threats that they weren't already doing. The gap between awareness and capability is where real exposure lives.

Every operator who deploys or travels to a threat environment carries a personal device that has spent years accumulating data about their habits, location history, contacts, and identity. That device is not neutral. It broadcasts, syncs, and logs in ways that are invisible to the person carrying it and valuable to an adversary with basic collection capability. A one-hour annual briefing does not change any of those behaviors.

## What Compliance Training Does Not Cover

The standard personal OPSEC curriculum teaches operators to avoid posting sensitive information on social media, to use strong passwords, and to be cautious about phishing. These are not wrong. They are also not sufficient for an environment where collection is active, persistent, and automated.

What most training does not cover:

Commercial applications request access to contacts, microphone, camera, location, and network information as a condition of basic functionality. Operators accept these permissions without understanding that the data collected is sold, aggregated, and indexed in ways that make personal networks and patterns of life visible to anyone willing to pay for them or breach the data brokers that hold them.

Device operating systems (even stock and updated ones) maintain persistent identifiers that allow location tracking across different network environments and link different devices to the same person over time.

Personal credentials are routinely compromised in data breaches and then used to access associated accounts through credential stuffing, which is automated and requires no specific targeting of the individual.

Social media metadata (exif data in photos, timestamp and geolocation data, and the network graph visible from follows and connections) provides accurate pattern-of-life data without any active surveillance.

None of this requires sophisticated adversary capability. Much of it is accessible through commercial tools.
